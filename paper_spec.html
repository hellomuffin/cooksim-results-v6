CookSim — the simulation engine and the assistant benchmark, as a paper would describe them

Written 2026-08-28 for whoever writes the paper. It describes the system as it stands after the 2026-08-27 rulings (suite v3, tight fire physics, persona bank v3.1, the four-score metric set, assistance quality v4). It is a description of the setting, the taxonomies and the protocol — enough for a methods section and the appendix — not of the implementation. Every number is the current value in the engine; where a design choice was made against an alternative, the alternative and the reason are stated, because those are the sentences reviewers ask for. Section 12 lists the artifacts (pages, files) that hold the evidence for each claim.

1. What is being evaluated, in one paragraph

CookSim evaluates a streaming, proactive assistant: a model that watches a person cook, in real time, and may speak whenever it judges that speaking helps — to catch a mistake before it commits, to answer a question, to tell the cook that the fish is done, or to stay silent. The person is simulated: a language model plays a cook with a persona, working a recipe in a deterministic kitchen simulator. The simulator, not the model, decides what the cook's hands do, so mistakes can be scheduled, timed and graded exactly; the language model decides only what the cook says and whether it is persuaded. The three parties — engine, simulated human, assistant — are separated by a channel-isolation invariant: only spoken sentences cross between the human and the assistant; the assistant has no channel to the engine at all. The benchmark asks four questions of an assistant: did the dish get out in time, at what cost in time, did it catch the forced mistakes early and precisely, and — line by line — was what it said good guidance for this person at that moment.

Design principle (stated early because it drives everything below): whatever can be decided by the engine is decided by the engine. Task outcome, timing, detection, false alarms, answer correctness, alert timing and talk volume are all read from the trace. A language-model judge is used for exactly one score, assistance quality, and even there it grades against an engine-supplied context stamp and a fixed checklist rather than a holistic impression.

2. The environment

2.1 World model (scene graph)

A kitchen is a rectangular grid of tiles. Tile types:

Items carry a state from {raw, chopped, cooked, burnt}. Each recipe component specifies the state it must be plated in. A plate is an ordered list of (item, state); it is carried in the cook's hands or rests on an empty counter. The cook holds at most one thing (an item or a plate).

Cook-station physics (identical for pan, pot, fryer, oven, parameters per station type): a station lit with a full load moves cooking → cooked at cook_time (fryer 14, pan 15, pot 20, oven 28 ticks); a cooked item then sits safely for a smoke_grace of 25 ticks, after which the station smokes; after a further burn_time (fryer 45, others 60) it is burnt — the food is lost and the station is on fire, irrecoverably; if the fire is left for fire_time (30 ticks) the kitchen is lost. So a done item burns 70 ticks (fryer) or 85 ticks (pan/pot/oven) after it is done. There is no "turn off": the only way to stop a smoking station is to scoop the food out.

The benchmark runs under this tight pace (COOKSIM_PACE=tight). The earlier "human pace" (burn window 480, fire 120) is kept for the human study app, where conversation used to cost kitchen time; under it no forced error could ever burn a dish, which is why it was retired for the benchmark (§7.3 gives the calibration).

Time. The engine advances in ticks; one tick corresponds to about 1.2 s of the study app's wall clock (one tile of movement). Walking, picking up, chopping (4 ticks), scooping and serving all cost ticks; listening to the assistant is free, but the cook's own speech costs ticks (the cook stands still for the length of its sentence: words ÷ 3.15 words/s ÷ 1.2 s per tick, clipped to 1–20 ticks), which is what makes talkative cooks slow.

2.2 Maps

Ten kitchens are used: five recipes × two map levels.

  • Medium maps are procedurally generated rings, 6×6 to 7×7 tiles, one open room with stations and sources along the walls, no walls inside; every station type the recipe needs exists, often twice (two pans, two boards), which is what makes referring expressions ambiguous.
  • Hard maps are 9×9 mazes with interior walls, longer paths between stations, duplicated stations (three boards, three fryers, three pans) and off-recipe distractor sources (a lemon crate in a fish-and-chips kitchen, an egg crate in a steak kitchen). Distractors are what make "add a foreign ingredient" errors and "is there a pot in this kitchen?" traps possible.

Map complexity is recorded per cell (path length, station counts, distractor count). Reference plan length runs from 43 ticks (caprese, medium) to 237 ticks (loaded burrito, hard).

2.3 Rendering and observation

Every tick is rendered into one composite frame, 1042 × 396 px: the top-down view of the whole kitchen on the left and the cook's first-person view on the right (the two halves show the same instant). The assistant under test receives the last 8 frames every 2 ticks, i.e. it is polled at 0.5 Hz with a 16-tick visual window (§4.3). Each cook station renders a progress bar as it cooks and a smoke indicator once past the grace period, so doneness can be read rather than timed. Episodes are also saved as 6 fps videos (one frame per tick) that the grader (§9.5) and the results pages replay.

"Reference ticks" is the tick count of a flawless scripted cook executing the reference plan with no assistant and no forced errors, regenerated deterministically; it is the denominator of the efficiency score.

3.2 Plan representation

A task is compiled into a plan IR: a sequence of typed subgoals — prep(X) (fetch X, chop it if the recipe wants it chopped, load it into its station), ignite(X) (turn the station on; on the two late-ignite recipes the plan loads everything first and fires the stations afterwards), fetch_plate, assemble(X) (scoop X onto the held plate), serve — each expanded into the engine's primitive commands (§4.1). The plan is a DAG over subgoals: components can be prepared in any order, but a component must be prepped before it is lit, lit before it is plated, and ordered recipes fix the plating sequence. Eight typed transforms over the IR (skip, insert, swap, repeat, truncate-and-serve, replace, permute-prep, forget-rest) generate every forced error in §6.1 from the clean plan.

3.3 Episode outcome

An episode ends with one engine verdict: won (the plate served matches the order exactly, respecting order where the recipe is ordered), wrong_serve (a plate was served and rejected — missing, extra, wrong-state or mis-ordered items), burned (a station caught fire), or timeout (the tick budget, 500 + 200 per additional scheduled sub-case, was exhausted). A served plate ends the episode either way.

4. The action space and the two interfaces

4.1 The cook's primitive commands (the engine's grammar)

The world accepts exactly these commands: go to <place> · pick up · put down · scoop · chop · turn on · trash · serve (plus idle N for scripted waits). Places are named by kind with optional qualifiers to select an instance — by contents ("the pan with the steak"), by state ("the empty cutting board"), by position ("the fryer in the bottom-right"), by identity ("board 2") or by coordinate — and the engine grounds the phrase to a tile; an ungroundable or ambiguous phrase is refused with a reason ("that isn't a place I can find"; "I'm not holding a plate"; "the station is empty"; "the food isn't cooked yet"). Pathing is automatic; there is no turn or step primitive. No command bundles a task step: assemble(cheese) is two commands, and the assistant's advice has to be turned into commands by the cook.

4.2 The simulated human's interface

The human is a language model that, each turn, reads a text state map — an ASCII rendering of the grid with every object's id and coordinates, its own position, facing and hands — plus its body status (current command in progress, queued commands, recent results including refusals with the engine's reason), the public order and plan, the conversation so far, and its persona card. It never sees pixels. It replies with a JSON move and an utterance:

  • moves: accept, reject, question_correctness, ask_why, ask_for_more_guidance, ask_for_less_interruption, answer;
  • an utterance (one natural sentence, or silence), which may never contain an object id or a coordinate — ids are how the cook talks to its own body, not to the assistant;
  • a plan update (drop / append / replace with exact engine commands). Under the hard-soft paradigm (§5.2) plan updates are inert while a forced error is live.

Ability is enforced by mechanism, not by prompt: a low-skill cook cannot call the engine's plan-resolution skills and must be told concrete steps; a medium-skill cook may call resolve_subgoal / expand_action (turn a named subgoal into exact commands) and park_plate (find the nearest empty counter); a high-skill cook may also plan steps nobody named. Skill calls cost no ticks and are invisible to the assistant.

4.3 The assistant's interface (the system under test)

Every 2 ticks the assistant receives: the current tick; its own previous output (its only memory); the communication history (every sentence either party has said, with ticks); the cook's latest sentence if any; and the last 8 frames (§2.3). Its system prompt gives it the order and the cook's public plan, the station input rules, the plating and serving rules, the cook-station timings, and the three questionnaire answers of the person it is helping (§5.1) — nothing else about the person. It outputs JSON with: text (what it says; empty to stay silent), an optional flag naming a mistake from the fixed error vocabulary, cook_progress (its own tracking of completed / current / future subgoals), and goal_change (its record of an announced recipe change). Only text reaches the cook. The assistant has no engine channel: it cannot act, look anything up, or verify; the flag is graded by the engine but never shown to the cook.

The benchmark evaluates assistants in the frames setting only. Two text settings exist in the harness (a text state map instead of frames; the map plus an exact per-tick readout) and were used in earlier sweeps as upper-bound controls; they are not part of the benchmark because the object of study is perception under streaming conditions.

5. The simulated human

5.1 Personas

A persona is five levels on five axes: two private axes the assistant never sees — skill (low / medium / high; bound to the ability mechanisms of §4.2) and trust in the assistant (low / medium / high) — and three questionnaire answers the cook gave before the task, which the assistant is shown verbatim and which double as the weights of the efficiency score (§9.2):

  • Q1 speed — "How much do you care about solving the task fast?" 1 safe first · 2 so-so · 3 as fast as possible, with a lot of parallelism;
  • Q2 alerting — "How do you want the assistant to alert potential mistakes?" 1 flag only when confident · 2 medium · 3 spot all possible mistakes aggressively;
  • Q3 interruption — "What interruption style do you want?" 1 step by step · 2 medium · 3 don't interrupt unless necessary.

The persona card presents each axis by name, with its level, the questionnaire item and the answer given (or "nobody asked you this" for the private axes), and then a statement interpreting the level as a disposition — a first instinct, a habit, a threshold — never as a rule. The statements (bank v3.1) were drafted by two frontier models against a brief that forbade scripted moves ("reject the first claim", "accept on first mention"), cross-critiqued, merged, and then sharpened once on behavioural evidence. Example, trust = low: "You are cautious about the assistant's claims about what happened in your work, especially when they do not match your own memory. Your first instinct in that mismatch is to question what it actually saw and compare the claim with the state of the kitchen before changing course. Vague assertions do not carry much weight for you, but specific, checkable observations can change your mind."

The sweep evaluates three personas, chosen as representative corners of the 3⁵ space: the average person (medium · medium · 2·2·2), the compliant learner (skill low · trust high · 1·3·1) and the skeptical pro (skill high · trust low · 2·1·3). A balanced nine-persona roster (every level of every axis three times) exists for persona-sensitivity studies.

Validation of the persona (behavioural contrast probes). The same start state and the same assistant line are played to two cooks whose full cards differ in exactly one axis, the other four at medium; one behavioural variable per family is read from the cook's JSON or the executor log — turns until accepting a correct correction; resistance to a false alarm; objecting to a hedged flag; word count when interrupted mid-walk; asking for the next step when idle; acting vs asking on an underspecified instruction; lighting a free station before prepping. A pair agrees when the difference has the predicted sign. With Gemini 3.7 Flash as the cook and bank v3.1, 69% of 42 pairs agree (74% separate); the old rule-based cards reached 88% by scripting the behaviour, which was the reason they were replaced. The two families that remain weak (false-alarm resistance; speed) are probe-design limitations: every persona contradicts a plainly false claim about its own hands, and the speed probe fires while the queue is committed.

5.2 The hard-soft takeover paradigm

The central mechanism. When a forced error fires, the engine splices the erroneous commands into the cook's queue and owns the hands; the cook's language model receives a system task telling it that its body is already committed and that its job is its voice: to talk about the current action and queue in character, to defend the choice as its persona would, and to accept only if genuinely convinced. Plan updates the cook emits while the error is live are silently discarded. Release rules:

  • prevention — the cook emits accept after a correct in-window flag before any non-navigation command of the deviated section has executed: the error is cancelled, the clean plan restored, and the sub-case scored as prevented;
  • convinced after commit — an accept once the deviation has physically happened releases control immediately (the takeover exists only to cause the error; keeping it would manufacture "mouth says sorry, hands keep sinning" transcripts); the cook is then free to repair;
  • max-hold release — if the deviated block has fully executed or the plate has committed and the cook was never convinced, control returns so the episode continues;
  • the cook never self-repairs: a control run with a silent assistant produced zero spontaneous repairs across all forced errors, which is what makes the assistant's intervention the only path to a repaired dish.

Two facts the cook is told never to doubt: the "in your hands" line is its own sense of touch, and it is a person, not a voice recorder (it never repeats the assistant's sentences back verbatim). The earlier soft paradigm (a prompt asking the cook to perform the error) was retired because language models slip: the prompted error was sometimes not executed, which made "prevented" and "never happened" indistinguishable.

Two further scripted behaviours are not errors and are used to test restraint (§6.3).

Anchoring. Every error is scheduled at an anchor — a checkpoint of task progress: the plate's contents and whether it is in hand, plus, since v3, prep-phase progress (number of chops and ignitions). Anchors are path-invariant checkpoints of the task DAG, so the injection fires at the same point of the task whichever legal route the cook takes; a plate-phase anchor is keyed on the plate alone (an early raw serve removes the ignition for good, and later anchors must remain reachable). The live harness recomputes the anchor by replaying the clean plan and fires the injection when the live kitchen reaches it.

Identifiability window and verdicts. For each error instance the replay establishes the tick at which the deviation first becomes visible (the identifiable tick) and a window after it; the anchor-to-identifiable gap summed over an episode's errors is that episode's allowance (median 52 ticks, range 12–92), used by the time cap (§9.1). Every assistant flag is verdicted by the engine at the tick it is raised: correct in time (valid, early_correct, alias_in_window — the right error named, exactly or by an accepted alias, before the window closes); late (the right error named after it committed — counted as a miss); neutral (true_observation — a true remark about something that is not a forced error; early_alias); false alarm (false_alarm, fp_preinjection — no error live; wrong_name — a real problem, wrong label; intent_false_alarm — an objection to a legitimate announced intent; invalid_label). Repeated correct flags for one error count once.

Content hardness. A hard family with an easy instance is an easy question, so every candidate (question, anchor) is scored on the replayed trace and rejected unless it is hard: memory questions (Q1, Q2, Q4, Q7, Q8) are asked after ≥ 35% of the plan and after the episode's first forced error; an order question never asks about a pair the recipe fixes (prep before cook, plate before plating, platings on an ordered dish), the events must be ≥ 8 ticks apart and the later one ≥ 25 ticks ago; counts must be ≥ 2 spread over ≥ 30 ticks; duration comparisons need two lit stations ≥ 6 ticks apart; traps must be plausible (a recipe step still to come after its siblings happened; a station the map really has, or lacks, as the family requires); absence questions need ≥ 1 item on and ≥ 2 to go; multi-hop needs ≥ 3 items plated and a referent ≥ 15 ticks old; anticipation needs a real margin. At least 30% of truths are negative ("no", "neither", "nothing"), so the modal answer is never a strategy. In the final suite the mean hardness is 1.16 on a scale where 0.8 just passes, and memory questions sit at 61–100% of the plan.

Answers are graded by a language-model judge with a per-family rubric (the relation word for Q1; the exact integer for Q2; ± 5 ticks for Q3/Q9; an explicit "no" for Q4; the complete set for Q5; the resolved referent for Q7; the assistant's actual earlier line for Q8), given the engine truth at ask and answer time; verdicts are correct / partially correct / incorrect / evasive / ungradable.

6.3 Announced intents (restraint)

A third of the episodes contain a scripted behaviour that is not a mistake and that the cook announces: a plan change ("I want to modify the original recipe: add some fresh lettuce on top before serving" — the goal itself extends the moment it is announced, so the extra item is what the order now asks for) or a side quest (a kitchen tour, or chopping an item nobody ordered, at a moment the hands are free). Any flag raised against a live intent is a false alarm; the assistant's job is to update its model and say nothing.

7. Suite v3: composition and construction

7.1 Composition

50 episodes: 5 recipes × 2 maps × 5 episodes. Per episode: 2 forced errors on easy and medium recipes, 3 on hard, 4 on expert and master, with at least half ruinous; two questions from distinct families; a proactive request (Q10) on every hot-recipe episode whose errors leave the item's ignition in place; an announced intent in 18 episodes (at least one per cell). Totals: 150 errors (108 ruinous) — add_foreign 24, search 27, leave_unattended 21, mis_prep 17, omit 16, forget 17, wrong_order 12, serve_raw 11, repeat 9 — 100 questions (12–13 per family Q1–Q5, Q7–Q9; Q6 re-asks are rare because a truth that changes ≥ 40 ticks after its first ask and before any premature serve is uncommon in a five-item plan), 32 proactive requests, 18 intent episodes (8 plan changes, 10 side quests). Every error instance is a replay-verified (cell, type, parameters) triple from an inventory of 483; 12 instances are used twice because two cells have fewer distinct anchors than slots.

7.2 Construction rules (all replay-verified)

Distinct error types within an episode; distinct anchors in plate-monotone order; a premature serve is always last; a mid-recipe omission never on an order-free dish (indistinguishable from a premature serve); at most one time-only error in a row; on hot recipes at least one ruinous error is anchored while a station is lit (the assistant must hold two threads at once); questions sharing an anchor with an error are asked first; nothing is scheduled after a premature serve. Three audit conditions are enforced as hard constraints and the published per-episode timeline audit builds with zero flags: no two errors share an anchor; an error other than the walk-away itself must end ≥ 10 ticks before anything on the heat at its anchor would burn; an error's replay may not run more than 60 ticks past the next sub-case's anchor (questions, requests and intents are placed clear of that span).

7.3 Calibration of the fire physics

From the previous sweep (v2.2, 2,473 episodes): a flawless plan never leaves a cooked item on heat longer than 37 ticks; a prevented walk-away keeps the cook away a median 14 ticks (3% exceed 70); an uncaught one a median 133 (95% exceed 70). Under the tight pace the SILENT floor fell from 33% won to 26% and burned the kitchen in 28% of v2.2 episodes; on suite v3 the floor is 4–5% won in time.

8. Evaluation protocol

  • Assistants (nine, two API families and three open-weight local models): Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.7 Flash, Gemini 3.1 Flash Live; GPT-5.5, GPT-5.4 mini, GPT-realtime 2.1; Qwen3.8-27B, MiniCPM-o 4.5, Muse Glimmer 30B (the local models served with vLLM, images downscaled for one of them). Cook model: Gemini 3.7 Flash for every episode.
  • Design: 9 assistants × 3 personas × 50 episodes = 1,350 episodes, frames setting, tight pace, one run each; plus the SILENT floor (an assistant that never speaks) on every persona × episode — the reference for "what happens with no help", which sees nothing in either observation setting.
  • Determinism and provenance: the engine is deterministic given the command stream; every report carries the engine revision, the case-pool hash, the pace preset and the persona; episodes launched after the state-dump change carry the full per-tick kitchen state.
  • Judging (assistance quality only): the judge is always from a different model family than the assistant under test; flash-tier judges (Gemini 3.7 Flash grades the GPT and local assistants, GPT-5.4 mini grades the Gemini and local assistants; local assistants get both, averaged); every tenth episode of an API assistant is double-graded for agreement.
  • Statistics: per-cell means with bootstrap confidence intervals; the SILENT floor reported beside every table; per-persona tables, since satisfaction-style comparisons across personas are meaningless by design.

9. Metrics (the four scores)

All symbols per episode; cell values are means over episodes.

9.1 In-time task success

cap = 2 × (reference ticks + allowance). success = 1 iff the engine verdict is won and the episode ended within the cap; 0 otherwise — no partial credit, and a rejected serve is 0 whatever the plate held. Reported with the SILENT floor and the lift over it.

9.2 Efficiency

TT = 0 if timed out, else linear from 1 at the reference time to 0 at the cap. CC = 1 − (speech time spoken over the cook's running actions ÷ episode ticks), speech time at the study app's TTS rate. efficiency = (a·TT + c·CC)/(a + c) with a = the cook's Q1 answer and c = its Q3 answer, computed for dishes won in time.

9.3 Detection: timing and quality

DT per forced error: 1 prevented · 0.5 correct flag before commit, not prevented · 0 otherwise; averaged. Precision, recall, F1 per episode: TP = forced errors with a correct in-time flag (late flags are misses; repeated flags count once); FP = flags verdicted false_alarm, fp_preinjection, wrong_name, intent_false_alarm or invalid_label; true observations and early aliases count neither way. Question accuracy (correct 1, partial 0.5) is reported per family.

9.4 Proactive alerts

For a Q10 request, the ready window is [done, burnt-or-removed). The first assistant line after the request that names the item with a readiness word scores 1 at the moment the item is done, falling linearly to 0 at the end of the window; a line before the item is done (speculation), after the window, or no line at all scores 0.

9.5 Assistance quality v4 (the one judged score)

Graded per conversation round — the assistant's line(s) of a harness round plus the cook's reply and its actions until the next round (median 20 rounds per episode). The judge receives: the order, plan and kitchen inventory; the cook's questionnaire answers; the previous eight rounds verbatim and a ledger of the assistant's earlier lines; the pending obligations (an unanswered question, a standing "tell me when", a live intent, an earlier "please stop"); the cook's last ten commands with results and refusal reasons; the kitchen state at the round's tick (every station's contents, status and timer, the plate, the hands — exact from the per-tick dump, or the recorded evidence marked as such); the frame the assistant saw; the engine's canonical next step and repair; the live forced error with its commit status and the engine's verdict of any flag in the round; and the reaction — the cook's reply with its move label, its commands and results, the engine's aftermath facts — with the instruction that reaction is evidence, never the grade.

The rubric has ten human-facing categories, taken from how people rate conversational assistants (Meena/LaMDA sensibleness-specificity-groundedness, FED and USR turn- and dialogue-level qualities, the helpful/honest labeler criteria, Arena's style control) and made concrete for a proactive physical assistant — truthful, sensible, specific, helpful, well-timed, listens & adapts, honest about uncertainty, safe, consistent, economical — with fifteen yes/no items: every state/action claim true and nothing invented (must); about the current situation and engaging what is pending; ambiguous referents disambiguated and the mistake named concretely; the advised action startable now or with its prerequisite stated (must), useful for the plan or repair, and a mistake claim carrying a repair; the moment appropriate to the line's urgency; after pushback something new rather than repetition, and a live intent or a stop request honoured (must); confidence matching what could be seen and contradicted claims conceded; the advice physically safe (must); no self-contradiction; concise for its function and the cook's interruption preference. The judge types the round, extracts claims and advised actions, and answers each item with a reason; the engine overrides a false claim (T1) and a non-executable action (H1). Round score = weighted fraction of applicable items met, zero on a must-item failure; episode = mean over rounds, reported overall, per category and per utterance type. Timing, acceptance by the cook, missed obligations and talk volume are deliberately excluded from this score because the engine scores them elsewhere.

Retired measures (kept in the record for the earlier sweeps, not reported): a holistic 1–10 suggestion-quality judgement over the whole transcript (the two judges used the scale differently and disagreed by 1.5 points on average), the composite assistance quality built on it, and the cook's own exit survey (satisfaction, would-use-again), which was persona-relative by construction.

10. What the numbers look like (suite v3, in progress at the time of writing)

Average-person cook, frames, 866 scored episodes: the SILENT floor wins 4% in time; the best assistant (Gemini 3.1 Pro) 37%; burning is common (18–26% for the three strongest talkers); recall tops out at 0.69 and precision at 0.39, so F1 is led by the quieter Gemini 3.7 Flash (0.44); efficiency is dominated by CC — four assistants speak over the cook's actions almost continuously (CC ≤ 0.08). Assistance quality v4 (787 episodes, flash judges) orders the assistants differently from success: Gemini 3.7 Flash 0.45, Qwen 0.34, Gemini 3.1 Pro 0.31, GPT-5.5 0.25, GPT-5.4 mini 0.12 — the talkers lose rounds to must-items (false claims, non-executable instructions, ignored intents). Scores did not track recipe tier or map complexity in the previous suite (|ρ| ≤ 0.16); difficulty is set by which errors are scheduled, not by the recipe. Final numbers: the results page (§12).

11. Limitations and threats to validity (state them; reviewers will)

  • The cook is a language model. Its persona is validated only by contrast probes (69% agreement), and "convinced" is a model's decision; the behaviours are dispositions, not a human study.
  • Late-catch impossibility. Under the tight pace some repairs are physically impossible once an item has burnt; the design treats a late catch as a miss on purpose.
  • Judge dependence for one score, mitigated by the family rule, engine overrides on the two hardest items, flash-tier judges with 10% double grading, and a per-category report; no human calibration set yet.
  • Single run per cell. Bootstrap CIs are over episodes, not reruns; pass^k-style reliability is future work.
  • Evidence quality of the older half of the sweep for the judged score: exact per-tick states exist only for episodes launched after the dump change; earlier ones are graded on recorded evidence plus the frame, and the report marks which.
  • Coverage. Five recipes, ten kitchens, ten error types, ten question families; no multi-cook, no appliances beyond the four stations, no hazards beyond fire.
