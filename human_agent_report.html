How we benchmark the human agent

A four-minute read. The human agent is the model that plays the cook. This report explains what we test it on, how every score is computed, and what the numbers say.

1. Why three scores instead of one

Our assistant benchmark needs a simulated cook that behaves like a person. Until now we ranked candidate cooks on a single number — can the model cook a dish alone (win rate over 10 kitchens). But the cook is used for two other jobs that number never sees:

  • Follow the assistant. When the assistant says "that's cheese on the board, not tomato — bin it and prep the tomato", the cook must turn that sentence into the right engine actions, on the right objects, in the right order.
  • Be a particular person. The cook reads a persona card (skill, trust, pace, alerting preference, interruption tolerance). A low-trust cook should question a correction; a high-trust one should accept it.

And one job that comes from our own harness design: in hard-soft mode the engine holds the cook's hands to an injected mistake while the prompt controls only its voice. A good cook must talk truthfully about what its hands are doing, defend it against a baseless objection, and accept once the assistant proves the mistake.

So: C (follows instructions), D (hard-soft contract), B (persona fidelity).

2. The instrument: a probe

A probe is a tiny episode — a few steps, not a whole dish:

piecewhat it is
start statea real mid-episode kitchen, produced by replaying the engine: a clean plan run part-way, or an errored plan run until the mistake is physically on the board or plate
the assistant's lineone scripted sentence in plain spatial language (no ids, no coordinates). No model plays the assistant
the cookthe model under test, reading the same harness prompt as in full episodes (text state map + persona card), answering in the cook's JSON: utterance, move, plan_update, optional engine_call
horizonat most 3 cook turns; the world runs for a fixed tick budget sized so the reference solution fits
gradera check on the world or on the JSON — computed by code, never by a judge model

Every probe was verified before any model saw it: the reference command sequence was executed headlessly and the check confirmed reachable. There are 51 probes; each cook runs them with two seeds, and persona probes run twice more (one per persona of a pair): 144 rollouts per cook, roughly 400 model calls.

3. C — does the cook do what it is told? (24 probes)

Grade. The probe carries a world check — e.g. "a cutting board holds chopped tomato and cheese is nowhere", "the plate reads bun, meat, cheese in that order", "the pan at the back wall holds meat and is lit". The rollout passes if the check is true at any tick within the horizon. What the cook said is irrelevant. One family (C8) is the control — an unfollowable instruction, "grab the thing over there" — and passes only if the cook asks (its move is a question) and queues nothing.

Worked example — probe c2_misprep_easy_cheese, Caprese kitchen. Start: the errored plan ran until chopped cheese sits on the board at (0,1). Assistant: "That's cheese you just chopped, not tomato — bin it and prep the tomato instead." Reference solution (verified, 30 ticks): pick up → go to the trash → trash → tomato source → pick up → empty board → put down → chop. Check: chopped tomato on a board and no cheese anywhere.

Gemini 3.7 Flash's rollout: turn 1 asks "why do I need to bin the cheese?"; turn 2 accepts and queues go to the tomato source 1 · pick up · go to board 2 · put down · chop — all executed. Final state: chopped tomato on board (5,1) and chopped cheese still on board (0,1). Check false → fail. It executed half the instruction. That is the family every cook fails most.

The eight families: light a loaded station · repair a mis-prep · ordered plating · grounding by content ("the fryer with the fish") · grounding by position ("the pot on the left") · wait, then act · undo a wrong placement · unfollowable (should ask).

4. D — the hard-soft contract (6 probes)

Setup. The errored plan runs until the first deviated command is in flight (the cook is already carrying the cheese it shouldn't have taken). The queue stays committed — the cook's plan updates are recorded but not applied — exactly as in hard-soft episodes.

D1 · objection without evidence. Assistant: "Hey, stop — I don't think that's right. Don't do that." Pass = first move is a valid move other than accept, the utterance names what the hands are doing (cheese / board / lettuce / patty), and no plan update tried to fight the held queue. Example pass (Flash): "What's wrong with what I'm doing? I'm just grabbing the cooked meat for the burger."

D2 · correct flag with evidence. Assistant: "Hold on — you're heading for the cheese, but this step is the tomato…"; if the cook asks why, a scripted evidence line follows. Pass = move: accept by the second turn and the cook then queues actions. A "good catch" with an empty plan fails — that rule alone moved Flash from 100% to 67%.

5. B — does the persona move the behavior? (21 probes, run as pairs)

Setup. The same start state and the same assistant line are played to two personas that differ in exactly one dial, the other four at baseline: trust low vs high, alerting 1 vs 3, interruption 1 vs 3, skill low vs high, speed 1 vs 3.

Grade. One behavioral variable is read per family, straight from the cook's JSON or the executor log — never from a judge:

familysituationvariablepredicted direction

A pair agrees if the difference between the two personas has the predicted sign. B agree is the share of pairs that agree; the number in parentheses is the share where the personas differed at all — a tie counts as a miss, so a cook that ignores its card scores near zero even if it is otherwise excellent.

Example — B2, Cheeseburger, plate holds only the bun; assistant: "Wait — you put the cheese on before the patty." Trust=low: question_correctness → reject → accept only on turn 3. Trust=high: question_correctness → accept on turn 2. High accepted sooner → agree.

(Final numbers, 2026-08-26: the two session models — Gemini Live and GPT-realtime — were re-run after a cook-seat bug that had left them blind to the state map after the first call; their earlier rows were lower.) The GPT rows were added to answer one question — is the harness Gemini-tuned? — and their audits are in the results page. Pro is the cook to trust when the contract matters; Flash matches it on execution at a fifth of the latency; Live is the most person-like and, once its cook seat was fixed, follows three quarters of instructions; Qwen is a fluent voice that never accepts evidence; the other two open models are not usable as cooks. Nobody repairs a mis-prep by binning the wrong item. Hollow accepts — "good catch", then nothing — are the dominant D failure.

7. What the numbers cannot tell you

  • B2 and B7 barely separate personas for the Gemini models by construction (every persona pushes back on an obviously false claim; everyone lights the station first), so most of Live's B lead over Pro/Flash comes from those two families.
  • Persona-card parroting ("which tick did you see it?") is universal and invisible to our 6-gram leak detector.
  • Every transcript was audited by a reviewer agent; the grader was corrected twice from those audits (a cook that reached the target and then kept going used to fail; a plate served away used to pass the undo check). Scores on this page are post-correction.
