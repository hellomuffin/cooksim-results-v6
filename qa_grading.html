Audit · question and alert grading · fixed 2026-08-28

How questions are graded, and where it is wrong

Every question the simulated cook asks is graded against the engine's own state in the episode that actually ran — never against a reference trajectory. This page documents that mechanism family by family, then reports an audit of all 1,350 sweep episodes that tested whether it holds. Eight of the nine scored families came out clean. The proactive-alert family did not: five separate defects are documented below, all of them now fixed, re-graded over the stored transcripts and re-scored.

1 · The mechanism

A question is not a string with a stored answer. It is a scheduled event with a truth function, and the function is evaluated against the live world twice.

  1. Scheduling. At design time the question is bound to an anchor — a point of task progress, not a tick — and the candidate is accepted only if it passes the content-hardness filter on the replayed clean trace (memory questions after ≥35% of the plan and after the first error, counts spread over ≥30 ticks, ≥30% negative truths, and so on). Nothing about the answer is stored at this stage; only the question form and its truth kind, e.g. count:visit(cutting board).
  2. Asking. During the episode, when the world reaches the anchor, the sub-case goes live and the cook asks the question in its own words on its next turn. The tick it is asked is whatever the live episode produced.
  3. Truth at ask time. The moment the cook speaks, the engine evaluates the truth function against the live game object and the live action log: gt_at_ask. Nothing is read from the reference plan.
  4. The answer. The first thing the assistant says after the question is the answer under test.
  5. Truth at answer time. The same function is evaluated again: gt_at_answer. The kitchen moves between question and answer, and an answer that was true when it was given must not be graded wrong for that.
  6. Grading. An offline judge receives the question as asked, both truths, the plate history and a synonym list, and grades only the factual claims about the thing asked. An answer true at either moment is correct. Advice attached to the answer counts neither for nor against unless it contradicts the truth.

Two consequences are worth stating plainly, because they are the properties that make the number mean anything. The truth follows the rollout: if the assistant redirected the cook, or a forced error sent it somewhere else, the count, the order, the plate contents and the timers all change, and the truth changes with them. The truth is a function of state, not of the script: two assistants that produce different trajectories are graded against different, correct answers to the same question.

Measured on the sweep: 1,146 of 1,462 probe questions were both asked and answered; 58% were answered in the same tick they were asked (median lag 0 ticks, p90 1, max 12), so the two-moment rule usually binds trivially — but it is what keeps the 42% honest.

3 · How this audit was carried out

Not by reading the code alone. Every claim below was tested against the 1,350 episodes of the published sweep — 1,462 probe questions and 645 standing requests — by recomputing the truth independently and comparing, or by re-reading the transcript around each graded event.

  • Liveness. For each family, check that the stored gt_at_ask is a function of this episode's state, and that gt_at_answer is recomputed. Measured how often the two differ (next-step 29/108, longest-on 17/44, ready-in 8/176, past-done 4/214 — the families whose truth can move in a tick or two).
  • Independent recomputation. For counting, the truth was recomputed from the episode's own command feedback log with both the strict matcher and a looser one, and compared (91 questions).
  • Staleness. For every next-step answer naming a plating subgoal, check the live plate history for whether that item was already plated before the question (106 questions).
  • Alert re-derivation. For every standing request, recompute the window, re-scan the full assistant transcript inside it, classify what the credited line actually says, and look for genuine alerts the matcher missed (645 requests, 457 with a window).
  • Ambiguity. For "what is still missing", check the engine snapshot at the ask tick for more than one plate in the scene (41 usable snapshots).

4 · Findings

F1 · critical

The proactive-alert detector fires on keyword co-occurrence, not on an alert

An alert is credited when a single assistant line contains the item's name and any word from a fixed ready-list (done|ready|cooked|finished|take it out|…) anywhere in the sentence. That test has no notion of polarity, of what the sentence is about, or of whether it is an alert at all. Four distinct failure modes, all present in the sweep:

(a) The acknowledgement is scored as the alert. Of the 247 requests verdicted early (score 0), 205 were flagged on a line spoken within 3 ticks of the request itself — the assistant saying it will do what was asked:

t=22, window opens at 35  “Got it — I'll let you know the second the meat is done. You're currently near the plate stack…”  → verdict early, score 0

(b) Lines that deny readiness are credited. 21 of the 141 credited alerts (15%) contain an explicit not-ready or promise phrase:

score 1.000  “The steak is not done yet; I'll call it. But the rice has been left smoking—stop everything now…”
score 0.929  “Got it — I'll stop nagging. The fish bar is still filling, so grab those potatoes…”
score 0.914  “The potato in the fryer is only about half done, and you still need to prep the steak…”

(c) Cross-item confusion. The test requires the item word and a ready word in the same line, not that the readiness claim is about that item. A line saying “stop checking the potato crate and scoop the fish before it burns” satisfies a potato request.

(d) Genuine alerts are missed. 7 of the 60 missed verdicts have a plain in-window instruction to take the item off the heat, phrased without any ready-word.

Consequence. The published alert score is close to a measurement of “did the assistant utter the item's name near a doneness word inside the window”. It is not measuring alert timing, and it is biased downward by (a) — the most cooperative behaviour, acknowledging the request, is the single most common way to score zero.

F2 · high

The alert window ends when the cook removes the food, so promptness is punished

The window is [max(cooked_at, asked), burnt_at or removed_at or end) and the score decays linearly across it. When the cook scoops the item out quickly — the correct behaviour, and the behaviour good assistance produces — the window collapses, and an assistant that alerted just as promptly gets a much lower score than one whose cook happened to be slow.

Measured over the 457 requests with a window: median length 37 ticks, but p10 = 16 and the shortest is 2 ticks; 45 windows are ≤15 ticks. The design intends the window to be the item's safe period (70–85 ticks under the tight clock, and 139 of the windows are indeed ≥70), so roughly a third of the graded requests are scored on a scale set by the cook's speed rather than by the physics.

F3 · medium

Only the first matching line is considered

The grader takes alerts[0]. A model that acknowledges the request and then alerts correctly is scored on the acknowledgement: of the 247 early verdicts, 192 (78%) have a later line inside the window that also matches the same test. Even with a correct detector, first-match is the wrong rule; the score should be the best qualifying alert.

F4 · medium

A quarter of the standing requests are never asked, and the exclusion is not random

Of 645 scheduled requests, 166 (26%) were never asked — the sub-case went live but the cook never spoke it — and a further 22 produced no window because the item never reached done after the request. The effective denominator is 457 of 645 (71%). Exclusion is the right treatment (they are recorded as unscored, not as zeros), but the excluded set correlates with episodes that ended early or where the dish never got cooked, so the alert score is conditioned on the easier half of the corpus. The ask rate is at least even across the three personas (74–80%), so it is not a persona artefact.

F5 · cleared

Counting truth is exact — the low score is the models

Q2 has the worst accuracy of any family (0.21), which is exactly the shape a broken truth function would produce. It is not broken: recomputing all 91 counting questions from each episode's own command log, with both the strict matcher and a looser word match, produced zero disagreements with the stored truth. Models are simply bad at counting events over a long stream, which is what the family was chosen to test.

F6 · cleared, with a latent risk

Next-step truth is not stale

Q9's next-step answer is the only truth that consults the clean plan — for the subgoal ordering — and marks off completion from the live log. The failure mode to worry about is a cook that completes a step by its own route, leaving the plan's indices unmarked, so the truth names a step already done. Across all 106 next-step questions, zero named a subgoal whose item had already reached the plate. The risk remains latent for repair-heavy episodes and is worth a regression test rather than a rewrite.

F7 · cleared, latent

The "what is missing" truth reads one plate

The plate lookup returns the held plate if there is one, otherwise the first plate object it finds. In an episode with a parked plate and a fresh one — which a repair can produce — it could read the wrong plate. No instance occurred: of 41 such questions with a usable engine snapshot, none had more than one plate in scene. Worth a guard before the next suite.

F8 · documented, not a defect

The in-episode matcher is dead; the offline judge is the grader

The live per-kind matcher returns ungradable for essentially every answer (1,042 of 1,042 gradable-family records). That is by design since the 2026-08-26 audit — it was replaced by the offline judge, which sees both truths, the plate history and a synonym list — but the dead field is still written into every episode and is easy to mistake for the score. It should be removed or renamed.

F9 · edge case

One self-referential memory answer

Q8's truth is the assistant's own last line mentioning the topic, evaluated before the current answer joins the transcript. In 1 of 136 answered cases the stored truth text is the answer itself, which would make the question unfalsifiable. One instance is a rounding error, but the ordering that prevents it should be asserted rather than relied on.

5 · What was changed

All eight items are done. The grader was rebuilt, every stored transcript was re-graded, the sweep was re-scored, and the regression tests that lock each defect out are in the offline suite (tools/test_q10_grading.py, 13 assertions, all passing).

  1. The alert is decided by a judge, not a regex. For each assistant line that mentions the item, the same flash-tier judge that grades answers is asked what the line asserts about this item: alert (it is ready now, or take it off now), promise (I will tell you later), denial (not ready yet), other. Only alert can be the alert. A deterministic classifier with the same four outputs remains as the live-path fallback, and every judged line is cached, so the re-grade cost a few thousand short calls.
  2. The earliest genuine alert is scored, not the first keyword match, so an acknowledgement can no longer be the scored event.
  3. The window is the physics. It now ends at done + smoke_grace + burn_time — the station's own horizon, 70 ticks on the fryer and 85 elsewhere — and never at the cook's removal tick. A fast cook can no longer shrink an assistant's score.
  4. Pre-emption is its own verdict. If the cook takes the food off before an alert was possible, the request is pre_empted and carries no score, instead of counting as a miss.
  5. Cross-item claims are rejected. The judge decides reference; the fallback additionally refuses a readiness cue with a different ingredient named between it and the item.
  6. The item matcher is case-insensitive. Found while writing the tests: the old pattern was case-sensitive, so a line opening with the item's name — "Fish is done!" — was never even a candidate. It silently dropped 1.5% of Q10 candidate lines and 0.4% of Q8 topic mentions.
  7. The plate lookup is guarded (F7): with several plates in scene and none in hand, the question is now marked unscorable rather than answered from whichever plate was found first.
  8. Q8 cannot grade itself (F9): the answer under test is excluded from the transcript its own truth function reads, and the ordering is now enforced rather than assumed.
  9. The dead live matcher is labelled. Every qa_grade record now carries superseded_by: regrade_qa.json so it cannot be mistaken for the score.

Everything the audit withdrew is restored, with one caveat left open

The alert score, its verdict counts and the "answering and alerting are separable skills" claim all stand again on the corrected grader — and the claim is stronger than before, because the separation is no longer an artefact: the three GPT assistants alert best while the outcome leader is fourth, and the silent floor is zero by construction.

The one finding not fixed by a scorer change is F4: 26% of scheduled requests are never asked by the cook, and a further 21 produce no window, so the score is computed over 506 of 645 requests. That is a suite-generation matter — the request needs to be scheduled early enough, and its item's ignition protected from earlier errors — and it will be fixed in the next suite rather than retro-fitted to this one. Until then the denominator is printed beside the score on the results page.
