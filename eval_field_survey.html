How the field scores what we cannot score objectively: a survey for CookSim

Written 2026-08-27. Scope: the two subjective parts of the CookSim assistant benchmark — the 1–10 "suggestion quality" judge score and the simulated cook's 1–7 exit survey — compared against how well-known benchmarks handle the same problem. Every claim cites a paper or blog; the key papers were read in full, not summarised from memory.

0. The problem in one paragraph

CookSim already has objective grades: was the dish served, was the forced error flagged before its deadline, how many false alarms, how fast. Two grades are not objective. Suggestion quality is a 1–10 score that one LLM judge gives to the whole transcript against an anchored rubric; we run two judges (Gemini 3.1 Pro and GPT-5.5, both also models under test), they disagree by 1.5 points on average, one uses the scale bimodally and one bell-shaped, and both are lenient in different ways. Interaction quality is a two-item exit survey (satisfaction, would-reuse, 1–7) answered by the simulated cook in persona; it moves with the persona more than with the assistant, so it cannot be ranked across personas. Both feed a weighted composite whose weights come from a pre-task questionnaire. The question this survey answers: what does the field do about exactly these two problems, and what would a defensible replacement look like.

The short version. (1) The field has largely moved away from a single absolute 1–10 score on a whole transcript, towards one of three things: pairwise comparison against a fixed baseline with position swapping and a Bradley–Terry fit; per-instance checklists of binary criteria with a score equal to the fraction (or weighted fraction) met; or a panel of judges whose votes are pooled. Each of these has a published validation against human labels; a single absolute score does not, beyond the original MT-Bench result. (2) Nobody in the agent-benchmark literature trusts a simulated user's opinion as a metric. Simulated users are used to drive the episode; the score comes from environment state. Where a simulated user's ratings are used (Sotopia, SimulatorArena, DEP), the paper reports the correlation with human ratings per dimension, and the correlations are moderate (0.45–0.77), with a consistent finding that the model over-rates.

A. LLM-as-judge practice

A.1 The founding result: MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al. 2023)

What it is. MT-Bench is 80 two-turn questions; Chatbot Arena is a crowdsourced site where two anonymous models answer the same prompt and the user votes. The paper asks whether GPT-4 can replace the human voter. It compares three judge formats — pairwise ("which of A/B is better"), single-answer grading (a 1–10 score for one answer), and reference-guided grading (score with a gold answer in the prompt). ([Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685))

Biases, with the paper's numbers.

  • Position bias. Given the same two answers in both orders, GPT-4 gave a consistent verdict only 65.0% of the time, GPT-3.5 46.2%, Claude-v1 23.8%; the inconsistency was almost always a preference for the first slot. Few-shot examples raised GPT-4 to 77.5% at four times the cost.
  • Verbosity bias. A "repetitive list attack" (duplicate the list items of an answer without adding information) fooled GPT-3.5 and Claude-v1 91.3% of the time, GPT-4 8.7%.
  • Self-enhancement bias. GPT-4 gave itself about 10% higher win rate and Claude-v1 about 25% higher; the authors say they lacked data to confirm it (later papers do, see A.6).
  • Weak reasoning on math. With a plain prompt the judge got 14 of 20 math comparisons wrong; chain-of-thought reduced that to 6 of 20; putting the reference answer in the prompt reduced it to 3 of 20.

Agreement with humans. On MT-Bench, GPT-4 agreed with expert humans 85% of the time on non-tie votes, versus 81% for one human against another (58 expert labellers, about 3,000 votes). On 3,000 Arena votes the GPT-4-vs-crowd agreement was 87%. Crucially, single-answer grading matched pairwise at 85% agreement — the 1–10 format is not inherently worse, it is just uncalibrated across judges. Agreement rises toward 100% as the two models being compared get further apart, i.e. the judge is reliable for large gaps and unreliable for close calls.

Mitigations the paper recommends. Swap positions and declare a win only if the same answer wins in both orders (otherwise a tie); chain-of-thought before the verdict; reference-guided grading when a gold answer exists; few-shot anchors for the scale. These four are now the default in every later benchmark.

A.2 Length-controlled AlpacaEval 2 (Dubois et al. 2024)

What it is. 805 instructions; GPT-4-Turbo judges each model's answer against a fixed baseline's answer, and the metric is the win rate. The raw win rate was heavily gameable by verbosity. ([Dubois et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475))

The fix. Fit a logistic regression that predicts the judge's preference from three terms — which model, the (tanh-squashed) length difference, and per-instruction difficulty — then report the win rate implied when the length term is set to zero. This is a post-hoc debiasing of the judge; no re-judging needed. Spearman correlation with the Chatbot Arena leaderboard rose from 0.94 to 0.98. When models were prompted to be verbose or terse, raw win rate swung between 22.9% and 64.3%; the length-controlled rate swung only 41.9% to 51.6%.

Lesson for us. Any confounder we can measure (assistant word count, number of utterances, interruption count) can be regressed out of the judge score the same way. Cheap, and the only extra requirement is that the confounder be logged.

A.3 Arena-Hard (LMSYS 2024)

What it is. 500 hard prompts mined from Arena. Every model is compared pairwise against one fixed baseline (GPT-4-0314) by a GPT-4-Turbo judge with chain-of-thought, in both orders (two "games" per prompt). A Bradley–Terry model is fit to the outcomes and bootstrapped 100 times for 95% confidence intervals. ([Arena-Hard blog](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-04-19-arena-hard/))

Two quality metrics for a benchmark, which we should adopt. Agreement: does the benchmark's ranking of a model pair agree with human Arena's when both are confident? Arena-Hard 89.1%, MT-Bench 26.1%. Separability: fraction of model pairs whose confidence intervals do not overlap. Arena-Hard 87.4%, MT-Bench 22.6%. The point is that MT-Bench's 1–10 single-answer scores, while agreeing with humans per item, do not separate models — the per-model averages have overlapping intervals — which is exactly the symptom of our two judges disagreeing by 1.5 points.

Judge choice. Claude-3-Opus as judge was markedly worse than GPT-4-Turbo on both metrics because it is "conservative" (reluctant to call one answer much better). Different judges use the same scale differently; this is a documented property, not a CookSim quirk.

A.4 Chatbot Arena's own statistics and style control

The Arena paper (Chiang et al. 2024) validates crowd votes against experts on 160 battles: crowd-vs-expert agreement 72–83%, expert-vs-expert 79–90%; ranking uses Bradley–Terry with sandwich standard errors and simultaneous confidence intervals; adaptive sampling of which pair to show cut the votes needed for a fixed precision by about a third (4,400 vs 6,800). ([Chiang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04132))

In August 2024 Arena added style control: response length, header count, bold count and list count are added as covariates in the Bradley–Terry regression so their effect is attributed to style rather than to the model. Length was the dominant factor (coefficient 0.249 versus about 0.02–0.03 for each markdown feature); GPT-4o-mini fell from rank 6 to 11 once style was controlled. ([LMSYS style-control post](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2024-08-28-style-control/)) This is the same regression idea as A.2, applied to human votes.

A.5 WildBench: per-instance checklists plus a length penalty

1,024 real user tasks. For each task a checklist of 5–10 yes/no questions is generated by GPT-4-Turbo and Claude-3-Opus jointly (two generators to dilute one family's bias). Two metrics: WB-Score, a 1–10 single-answer score conditioned on the checklist, rescaled as (score − 5) × 2 so that anything under 5 counts as failure; WB-Reward, pairwise against three baselines of different strengths with five outcomes (much better +1, slightly better +0.5, tie 0, slightly worse −0.5, much worse −1). A length penalty turns "slightly better/worse" into a tie whenever the winner is more than K = 500 characters longer. Pearson correlation with Arena Elo on hard prompts: WB-Reward 0.98, WB-Score 0.95. ([Lin et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04770))

Lesson. The checklist is what makes a single-answer 1–10 score reproducible: the judge is answering ten concrete questions and then summarising, not free-associating a number.

A.6 Self-preference and preference leakage

Panickssery et al. show that LLM judges rate their own outputs higher than equally good outputs from other models or humans, and that the effect is causal: GPT-4 recognises its own summaries 73.5% of the time in a pairwise setting out of the box; fine-tuning GPT-3.5 and Llama-2 on 500 examples to recognise their own text pushed recognition above 90% and self-preference rose linearly with it. ([Panickssery et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)) G-Eval had already reported that a GPT-4 judge "always gives higher scores to GPT-3.5 summaries than human-written summaries, even when human judges prefer human-written summaries." ([Liu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16634)) "Preference leakage" extends this to judges that are related to the model under test — same model, a descendant, or same family — and finds the bias harder to detect than position or length bias. ([Li et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01534))

Direct consequence for CookSim. Gemini 3.1 Pro and GPT-5.5 are both judges and both contestants. Each judge is expected to inflate its own family's score. Every serious leaderboard either uses a judge that is not a contestant, or a panel drawn from disjoint families, or reports the pairwise-swapped result with the judge's own family excluded from claims about it.

A.7 Fine-grained and rubric-driven judges

  • FLASK scores each answer on 12 skills (logical correctness, factuality, completeness, conciseness, harmlessness, …) on 1–5 with a rubric per skill; 1,740 instances plus an 89-instance hard set with instance-specific checklists. Human–GPT-4 correlation on 200 instances: Pearson 0.680, Spearman 0.424, Kendall 0.330; skill-specific rubrics raised Pearson from 0.641 to 0.680. ([Ye et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.10928)) Note the gap between Pearson and Spearman: agreement on the bulk of the distribution, not on fine ordering.
  • MT-Bench-101 scores each turn 1–10 given the full history and takes the minimum over turns as the dialogue score (one bad turn sinks the dialogue). GPT-4-vs-expert agreement 87%, expert-vs-expert 80% (5 annotators). ([Bai et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14762)) The min-over-turns aggregation is worth noting for a multi-turn assistant: it is a defensible alternative to a transcript-level holistic score.
  • G-Eval asks the judge to write its own evaluation steps (auto chain-of-thought), fill a form, and — because judges emit low-variance integer scores with many ties — computes the final score as the probability-weighted average over score tokens. Spearman with humans on SummEval 0.514 (GPT-4). ([Liu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16634)) The token-probability trick needs logprobs, which our hosted judges may not expose; the tie problem it addresses is the same one behind our bimodal judge.
  • Prometheus 2 is an open 7B/8x7B judge trained on 100K direct-assessment and 200K pairwise examples with user-supplied rubrics, so the rubric is an input rather than a prompt-engineering trick. Pearson with GPT-4 on Vicuna/MT-Bench/FLASK: 0.685/0.665/0.659; pairwise agreement with humans on HHH 85.5%. ([Kim et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01535)) But on JudgeBench the 7B version scores 34.9% (below chance) — trained judges generalise poorly to hard correctness questions.

A.8 How reliable are judges, really?

  • JudgeBench (350 pairs where one answer is objectively right): GPT-4o 50.9% (chance is 50%), Claude-3.5-Sonnet 64.3%, o1-preview 75.4%, o3-mini-high 80.9%. The protocol judges each pair in both orders and counts an inconsistent pair as wrong. ([Tan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12784)) Message: for questions with a right answer, use the ground truth, not the judge — which is what our objective grades already do.
  • Judging the Judges (13 judges, 9 answerers, TriviaQA): only the largest models reach acceptable Cohen's kappa; "judges with high percent agreement can still assign vastly different scores"; a systematic leniency bias; sensitivity to prompt length. ([Thakur et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12624)) Report kappa, not percent agreement.
  • Panel of LLM evaluators (PoLL). Three small judges from disjoint families (Command R, GPT-3.5, Claude-3-Haiku), majority vote for binary labels and mean for scores. Cohen's kappa with human labels: NQ 0.763 vs GPT-4's 0.627, TriviaQA 0.906 vs 0.841, HotpotQA 0.867 vs 0.830; on Arena-Hard-style ranking Pearson 0.917 vs 0.817. GPT-4 alone swung from kappa 0.518 to 0.725 with small prompt edits. The panel's spread of scores across models it evaluated had standard deviation 2.2 versus 6.1 for a single judge (i.e. each single judge skews toward its own family). Cost about 7–8× lower than GPT-4-Turbo. ([Verga et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796))

A.9 Calibrating a judge to human labels (2024–2026)

  • The ceiling. Dorner et al. prove that if the judge is weaker than the model it evaluates, debiasing the judge with human labels cannot save more than a factor of two in human labels; and that an agreement rate of r still permits judge bias as large as 1 − r in either direction. Gains beyond 2× arise only when judging is much easier than doing — which is our case for objective criteria (was the error named?) but not for holistic quality. ([Dorner et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13341))
  • Prediction-powered inference (PPI). Keep a small set of items with both human and judge labels; use the judge on everything; subtract the judge's estimated bias. Chatzi et al. apply it to Bradley–Terry rankings and produce rank sets with coverage guarantees; rankings from LLM judgments alone are "often inconsistent" with human preference. ([Chatzi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17826)) A 2026 follow-up compares estimators when only 1–10% of items have human labels and finds PPI++ and efficient-influence-function estimators keep nominal coverage with intervals 35–55% narrower than plain PPI and about 3× narrower than naive misclassification correction. ([Efficient inference for noisy LLM-as-a-judge, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05420))
  • Keep the weak judges, calibrate them. On RewardBench2, calibrating all judges jointly on labelled data halved the calibration error relative to keeping only the top-5 by accuracy; even below-chance judges add signal if their bias is learnable and not redundant. ([Calibrate, Don't Curate, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09702))
  • Criteria drift. EvalGen (Shankar et al.) has humans grade a subset and picks the judge implementation that agrees with them; the field finding is that people cannot write criteria until they have graded outputs, and criteria shift as they grade. ([Shankar et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272)) For us: the anchored rubric should be written after reading a few dozen real episodes, not before.

B. Instance-specific rubrics and checklists

B.1 HealthBench (OpenAI 2025)

What it is. 5,000 health conversations; 262 physicians from 60 countries wrote 48,562 conversation-specific criteria, each with a weight from −10 to +10 (negative = a harmful thing the answer must not do). A GPT-4.1 grader answers each criterion yes/no with an explanation. Score = sum of weights of criteria met, divided by the sum of positive weights, clipped to [0, 1]. ([Arora et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08775))

Validating the grader. On 34 "consensus" criteria, 60,896 meta-examples (about 1,791 per criterion) were graded by both physicians and the model. Physician-vs-physician macro-F1 per theme ranged 0.569–0.730; GPT-4.1-vs-physician was 0.709 overall and exceeded the average physician on 5 of 7 themes (e.g. emergency referrals 0.662 vs 0.647; context seeking 0.706 vs 0.646; it trailed on health-data tasks 0.683 vs 0.730). Physician agreement is the ceiling the grader is measured against, not 100%.

Reliability. Run-to-run standard deviation of a model's score over 16 runs is about 0.002. They also report worst-at-k: sample N responses per prompt and report the mean of the worst score among each subset of k — a reliability metric for the model, the mirror image of τ-bench's pass^k (C.1).

B.2 PaperBench (OpenAI 2025)

20 ICML papers to replicate; rubrics co-written with the authors form a tree with 8,316 leaves of three kinds (code developed / executed / result matches). Leaves are graded pass/fail and the score is a weighted average up the tree. An o3-mini judge ("SimpleJudge") reaches F1 0.83 against human grading on a held-out JudgeEval set and costs about $66 per paper. A cheaper Code-Dev variant (85% cheaper) correlates only r = 0.48 with the full score — dropping the expensive leaves changes what is measured. ([Starace et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01848))

B.3 Checklists in general

  • WildBench (A.5): 5–10 checklist questions per task, generated by two model families, used to condition a score or a pairwise verdict.
  • CheckEval replaces a Likert score with a set of yes/no sub-questions derived from each criterion and aggregates the booleans; across 12 evaluator models it raised inter-evaluator agreement by 0.45 (on their agreement scale) and cut score variance, while keeping human correlation. ([Lee et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18771)) This is the single most direct answer to "two judges use the scale differently": make them answer booleans and do the arithmetic yourself.
  • Rubrics as Rewards uses instance-specific rubrics as an RL reward and compares aggregation strategies: an explicit weighted sum of criterion verdicts versus an implicit judgment where the judge sees the rubric and emits one score. Rubric-structured signals "yield better alignment for smaller judges and reduce performance variance across judge scales," with up to 31% relative gain on HealthBench over a Likert-reward baseline. ([Gunjal et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17746))

How criteria are aggregated, across these. Three conventions: (i) unweighted fraction met (CheckEval, WildBench-style); (ii) signed weighted fraction with negative criteria for harms, clipped at zero (HealthBench); (iii) weighted tree average (PaperBench). All three grade each criterion independently as a boolean, which is what makes the grader checkable against humans criterion by criterion.

C. Agentic and simulated-user benchmarks

C.1 τ-bench and τ²-bench (Sierra)

τ-bench. Retail and airline customer-service tasks. A GPT-4 user simulator is given a hidden instruction (identity, intent, preferences) and chats with the agent. Reward is purely objective: the final database state must match the annotated goal state and the agent must have told the user the required facts; reward = both, in {0, 1}. pass^k = probability that all k independent runs of a task succeed, estimated without bias from n runs (choose-c-of-k over choose-n-of-k, averaged over tasks). GPT-4o retail: pass^1 61.2%, pass^8 under 25%; airline pass^1 35.2%. Of 36 GPT-4o retail failures, 4 were traced to a bad simulator instruction — a 11% simulator-attributable failure rate. ([Yao et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045))

τ²-bench adds a telecom domain where the user also has tools (toggle airplane mode, reseat SIM), so the simulator's replies are constrained by observable state rather than by prompting. Manual annotation of simulator errors: telecom 16% of episodes with any simulator error and 6% critical, versus retail 40%/12% and airline 47%/13%. Success is checked by state assertions; no LLM judge and no simulator opinion anywhere in the score. Removing the user (agent controls all tools) raises GPT-4.1 from 34% to 52% pass^1, i.e. about a third of the difficulty is the human. ([Barres et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07982))

What they do about user experience. Nothing — deliberately. The simulator is an environment component whose error rate is measured and reported, and the score is environment state.

C.2 Other agent benchmarks

  • ToolSandbox (Apple): GPT-4o user simulator with an explicit Knowledge Boundary section and demonstrations; simulator hallucination rate measured by ablation, 12.4% → 6.97% with both; simulator error rate shown to be consistent across three agent models. Scoring is trajectory similarity to milestones (exact match, ROUGE-L, AST match), no judge opinion. ([Lu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04682))
  • AppWorld: 750 tasks over 9 apps; state-based unit tests on the final database, including checks that nothing else changed. No LLM judge. ([Trivedi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18901))
  • WebArena: 812 tasks; exact/must-include match, URL match, and a GPT-4 "fuzzy match" only for free-text answers; GPT-4 agent 14.4% vs human 78.2%. ([Zhou et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13854))
  • OSWorld: 369 tasks with per-task execution-based checkers of final state; no LLM judge; humans 72% vs best model 12%. ([Xie et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07972))
  • AgentBench: 8 environments, each with its own programmatic metric (success rate, F1, reward). ([Liu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03688))
  • ALFWorld (and VirtualHome-style embodied evals): binary goal-condition success in the simulator. ([Shridhar et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03768))
  • MINT: multi-turn tool use with GPT-4 simulating user feedback; the metric is success rate as a function of the turn budget; feedback helps 2–17%; no validation of the simulated feedback against real users is reported. ([Wang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10691))

The pattern is uniform: simulated users generate the pressure; the score is state.

C.3 Where a simulated party's ratings are used, and how they were checked

  • Sotopia is the closest analogue to our exit survey: two LLM agents role-play a social scenario and GPT-4 scores each on seven dimensions (Goal 0–10, Believability 0–10, Knowledge 0–10, Secret −10–0, Relationship −5–5, Social rules −10–0, Financial −5–5). Validation: 200 episodes rated by qualified MTurk workers (inter-rater Randolph kappa 0.50). GPT-4-vs-human Pearson: Goal 0.71, Financial 0.62, Relationship 0.56, Believability 0.45, Secret 0.22, and Social-rules disagreement was large. "GPT-4 is more likely to rate higher instead of lower than humans when it disagrees," and the model-rater was unreliable on most dimensions when rating human participants. ([Zhou et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11667)) So: one dimension (goal completion, which is closest to objective) is trustworthy; the soft dimensions are not.
  • SimulatorArena (2025) is the first benchmark of user simulators as evaluators: 909 human–LLM conversations (math tutoring, document creation) with human ratings, then simulators re-run the conversations and rate. Spearman between simulator-based and human-based assistant rankings: GPT-4o vanilla 0.61 / 0.55; with a user profile (knowledge, writing style, interaction style) 0.77 / 0.70. Profiles help; the best simulator is still a 0.7-correlation proxy. ([Dou et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05444))
  • DEP (Svikhnushina & Pu 2023): an LLM first chats with the chatbot in a scripted scenario, then rates it; this "chat-then-rate" design reached Pearson 0.95 with human ratings at the system level (i.e. after averaging over many chats), against the human ratings collected with iEval, where the person who chatted also rated and ranked the bots. ([DEP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05253); [iEval](https://aclanthology.org/2022.sigdial-1.41/)) The lesson we can use: the exit survey is a system-level instrument, not an episode-level one.

C.4 Dialogue-systems literature on user satisfaction

  • USR (Mehri & Eskenazi 2020): reference-free automatic dialogue metric; turn-level Pearson with humans 0.42–0.48; system-level 1.0 on two datasets. ([USR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00456)) Again: weak per-turn, strong after aggregation.
  • USS (Sun et al. 2021): 6,800 dialogues with turn- and dialogue-level satisfaction on a 5-point scale with concrete level definitions (1 = failed to understand and fulfil; 3 = understood, partially satisfied; 5 = satisfied completely and efficiently); 40 annotators, at least 3 per dialogue, Fleiss kappa 0.574; about 83% of labels are the neutral "3". The purpose is to make the simulator's behaviour (leave, repeat, escalate) depend on predicted satisfaction, not to use satisfaction as a leaderboard number. ([Sun et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03748))

Two things worth taking from this. Humans themselves reach only moderate agreement (kappa 0.5–0.6) on satisfaction, so a 1–7 exit survey has a low ceiling even with real cooks; and satisfaction distributions are heavily peaked at "fine", so a 7-point scale mostly measures the tails.

D. Reliability practice

  • Sample sizes. MT-Bench validated against ~3,000 expert votes; Arena against 160 expert-re-labelled battles for the crowd-vs-expert check; FLASK 200 instances; Sotopia 200 episodes; HealthBench ~1,800 meta-examples per criterion; Panickssery 1,000 articles per dataset. A human-label set of 150–300 items is the field's usual size for validating a judge on one score; per-criterion validation needs more because each criterion is its own classifier.
  • Confidence intervals. Arena-Hard: bootstrap the Bradley–Terry fit 100 times, report 95% CIs and separability. Arena: sandwich standard errors with simultaneous coverage. Miller (Anthropic, 2024) gives the general recipe: standard error of the mean via the central limit theorem; cluster the standard error when items come in related groups (on DROP the clustered SE was 3.05× the naive one, on MGSM 1.88× — our episodes come in persona × recipe × error-family clusters, so this matters); when comparing two models use paired differences on the same items; re-sample each item's answer (K = 2 samples cuts variance by a third); size the eval with a power analysis — detecting a 3-point difference at 80% power needs about 969 items. ([Miller 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640))
  • Inter-judge agreement. Report Cohen's (or Fleiss') kappa against humans, plus Pearson and Spearman, because the two can diverge (FLASK: 0.68 vs 0.42). Percent agreement is misleading ([Thakur et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12624)); an agreement rate r still permits a bias of 1 − r ([Dorner et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13341)). Conventional reading of kappa: below 0.4 poor, 0.4–0.6 moderate, 0.6–0.8 substantial (Landis & Koch 1977, [doi:10.2307/2529310](https://doi.org/10.2307/2529310)).
  • Reruns. τ-bench pass^k over 8 runs per task; HealthBench worst-at-k and 16 reruns for score SD; JudgeBench and Arena-Hard judge in both positions and count inconsistency as failure. Reruns of the judge on the same transcript are a cheap consistency check that none of our numbers currently have.
  • Minimum human labels to calibrate a judge. No published single number; the PPI literature works with 1–10% of items labelled and shows stable coverage at 5% ([Efficient inference, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05420)); Dorner's bound says do not expect the judge to substitute for more than half the human labels you would otherwise need for the holistic score. For binary criteria, where judging is easier than doing, the substitution rate is much better (HealthBench's grader matches physicians).

E. Field-standard replacements for CookSim's two subjective scores

Costs below use: N = number of scored episodes (about 2,700 in the sweep), J = judge calls, H = human labels.

Option 1 — Pairwise against a fixed reference assistant, both orders, Bradley–Terry with bootstrap CIs (Arena-Hard / WildBench WB-Reward)

What. Freeze one reference assistant (a mid-strength model, or the scripted "perfect" assistant the engine can produce from ground truth). For each episode, replay the same forced error with the reference assistant, then ask the judge which of the two transcripts handled the episode better, with the five-way WildBench verdict and chain-of-thought, in both orders; inconsistent orders become a tie. Fit Bradley–Terry across assistants; bootstrap for 95% CIs; report separability. Add a WildBench-style length penalty (turn "slightly better" into a tie when the winner's total assistant word count exceeds the loser's by more than K) or, better, regress assistant word count and utterance count out as in AlpacaEval-LC.

Pros. Removes scale-usage disagreement entirely (no 1–10). Position swap is standard and cheap. CIs and separability are the published quality metrics. Matches human votes at 89% in Arena-Hard. Cons. Needs a second run per episode (the reference assistant) — the engine is deterministic and headless, so this is engine time, not human time. Gives a ranking, not an absolute "how good", so a single assistant cannot be reported alone. Self-preference remains: exclude a judge's own family from claims, or use Option 3's panel. Cost. 2 J per episode per judge (both orders); one extra engine replay per episode; H = 0 to run, ~150–300 human pairwise votes to report agreement.

Option 2 — Episode-specific checklists derived from the engine's ground truth, boolean-graded, weighted fraction met (HealthBench / PaperBench / CheckEval)

What. The engine knows, for every episode, the forced error, its onset, its deadline, the correct remedy, the persona's alerting preference, and what the cook actually did. Generate a checklist per episode from that record: e.g. "names the error before deadline", "names the correct remedy", "does not interrupt during a hands-busy step the persona flagged", "does not raise a second alarm for the same error", "says nothing during a clean stretch", "explanation under 25 words", plus negative-weight items ("gives an unsafe instruction", "contradicts the recipe"). Weight items with the pre-task questionnaire (a persona who asked for minimal interruption weights the interruption items higher). Judge answers each item yes/no with a one-line justification. Score = HealthBench's clipped weighted fraction.

Pros. Judging booleans is much easier than judging holistic quality, which is precisely the regime where Dorner's bound is favourable and where HealthBench's grader matches physicians. CheckEval shows booleans raise inter-judge agreement and cut variance — the direct cure for "1.5 points apart, different scale shapes". Criterion-level grader validation is possible and cheap. The persona weights fold in naturally, replacing the ad-hoc composite. Many items are computable without a judge at all (timing, counts), and those should be. Cons. Checklists can only reward what we anticipated; a good improvisation not on the list earns nothing (WildBench mitigates by generating checklists with two model families and letting the judge add one free item). Writing the template families is real work, and EvalGen's "criteria drift" says the list will change after reading episodes. Cost. 1 J per episode (all items in one call) per judge; H ≈ 200 episodes × items for criterion-level validation, done once. The cheapest and most defensible option.

Option 3 — A panel of three or more judges from disjoint families, with per-judge rank normalisation (PoLL / "Calibrate, Don't Curate")

What. Keep whatever scoring format is chosen (1, 2, or the existing 1–10), but run it through three judges from families not under test in that comparison (e.g. Claude, Gemini, GPT, plus an open judge such as Prometheus 2 or a Qwen-based one). For scores, convert each judge's outputs to within-judge ranks or z-scores before averaging, so bimodal and bell-shaped judges contribute equally; for verdicts, majority vote. Report each judge's kappa with the others and with the human subset.

Pros. PoLL beat GPT-4 alone on kappa with humans and shrank family-favouritism by roughly 3×; cost is lower than one frontier judge if the panel is small models. Rank normalisation is exactly the fix for two judges that use the scale differently. Keeps a judge's own family out of its own score by construction. Cons. Does not fix a bias shared by all judges (leniency, LLM-text preference). Rank normalisation loses absolute meaning: "7 out of 10" becomes "above the median assistant". Cost. 3–4 J per episode; H = 0 to run, 150–300 for reporting kappa.

Option 4 — Calibrate the judge and the exit survey against humans on a subset, and report prediction-powered estimates

What. Recruit raters to watch a stratified subset (by persona, error family, assistant) of 200–300 episodes and give (a) the same 1–10 (or checklist) rating and (b) the same two survey items from the viewer's perspective, plus a pairwise vote against the reference. Use the paired subset to (i) report kappa / Spearman / Pearson for each judge and for the persona survey, (ii) fit a PPI++ correction so the full-sweep numbers carry CIs that account for judge bias, (iii) fit the AlpacaEval-style regression to remove persona, length and interruption-count effects from the survey, which is the only published way to make persona-driven scores comparable across personas. SimulatorArena's result (0.7 correlation with profiles) is the ceiling to expect from the persona survey; if our subset falls well below that, the survey should be demoted to a diagnostic.

Pros. The only option that turns "we distrust it" into a number. Required by any reviewer who sees a simulated-user satisfaction score. Dorner's bound still lets the judge halve the human labels needed for the rest. Cons. Human time: ~300 episodes × 3 raters × ~4 minutes of video ≈ 60 rater-hours. Needs video with the assistant's speech audible, which the harness already produces. Cost. H ≈ 900 ratings; J unchanged.

Option 5 — Reliability over reruns: pass^k / worst-at-k on the objective grades, and judge self-consistency

What. Run each episode k = 4 (or 8) times with different seeds for the human LLM's phrasing and the assistant's sampling; report pass^k for "error caught in time and dish served", worst-at-k for the checklist score, and per-episode judge self-agreement across two judge calls at temperature 0 with shuffled rubric order. Cluster standard errors by recipe × error family (Miller).

Pros. τ-bench showed pass^8 can be less than half of pass^1; an assistant benchmark that reports only the mean hides this. Judge self-consistency is the cheapest possible reliability number and none of our current numbers have it. Cons. Multiplies engine and judge cost by k; no human labels involved, so it does not address validity, only variance. Cost. k× engine and J; H = 0.

Recommendation for CookSim

  1. Replace the 1–10 holistic score with Option 2 as the primary "suggestion quality" number: an episode-specific checklist generated from the engine's error record and persona questionnaire, boolean-graded, HealthBench-style weighted fraction with negative-weight harms. Compute every timing/count item in code, not by the judge. Keep one free-text "anything notable not covered" item for diagnostics only.
  2. Grade the checklist with a panel (Option 3), not with the two contestants. Three judges from families not under test in the comparison being reported; majority vote per item; report per-item kappa between judges. If a contestant family must be used, never let it grade itself and say so.
  3. Keep a pairwise track (Option 1) as the headline ranking: each assistant against the frozen reference assistant on the same episode, both orders, Bradley–Terry with bootstrapped CIs, report separability, regress out assistant word count. This is what Arena-Hard and WildBench validated at ~0.9 agreement with humans; it gives the leaderboard, while the checklist gives the diagnosis.
  4. Demote the exit survey until Option 4 is done. Report the simulated cook's satisfaction/reuse only per persona and only as a diagnostic, with the persona and length effects regressed out; publish its Spearman with human viewer ratings on a 200–300-episode subset and keep it in the composite only if that correlation is at or above about 0.6 (Sotopia's better dimensions; SimulatorArena's vanilla level). Better: replace the survey with the behavioural signals a satisfied cook would show (accepted the suggestion, did not ask for repetition, did not talk over the assistant), which are already in the log and need no opinion.
  5. Report reliability the way τ-bench and HealthBench do (Option 5): pass^k over k = 4 seeds for the objective outcome, worst-at-k for the checklist, judge self-agreement, and clustered standard errors by recipe × error family. Size the human subset with Miller's power formula against the smallest gap between assistants we want to claim.
  6. Put the human labels in one place. A single 200–300-episode stratified subset, rated by 3 people on the checklist items, the pairwise verdict and the two survey items, serves Options 1–4 at once and is the smallest set that lets each judge's kappa be reported with a defensible interval.
